# Writing style and rhythm extracted from three papers (Fukushima 2014 J Neurosci; 2015 R Soc Open Sci; 2015 Sci Rep)

Source texts: Introduction, Results and Discussion of the three papers (about 570 sentences), compared
with the current arXiv v2 draft (Introduction to Conclusion, 224 sentences).

## 1. Measured rhythm

| | J Neurosci 2014 | RSOS 2015 | Sci Rep 2015 | arXiv v2 draft |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| median sentence length (words, parentheticals removed) | 26 | 19 | 23 | 32 |
| sentences over 40 words | 19% | 1% | 6% | 30% |
| sentences under 15 words | 13% | 23% | 17% | 12% |
| sentences opening with "We" (per 200) | 12 | 20 | 15 | 7 |
| opening with "This/These" (interpretive closer) | 22 | 10 | 17 | 5 |
| opening with "Thus/Therefore" | 12 | 8 | 6 | 1 |
| opening with "To ..., we ..." (purpose first) | 6 | 5 | 3 | 0 |

The three papers alternate long descriptive sentences with short declarative ones; the draft runs at a
uniform 30-word density. The papers name the actor ("we"), state the purpose before the method, and close
paragraphs with an interpretive sentence; the draft mostly reports nominal facts ("The wrong-consensus rate
... falls ...") and leaves the interpretation implicit or packs it into the same sentence with colons and
semicolons.

## 2. The unit of writing: the four-beat paragraph

Nearly every Results paragraph in the three papers has the same shape.

1. Purpose or question. "To quantify the information about the stimuli coded in the evoked responses, we
   performed classification analysis ..." / "We next examined whether the difference in performance across
   stimuli found in Sector 4 might be related to categorical differences among the vocalizations." / "Could
   this small MPS region contain any cues for physical characteristics of the caller?"
2. Method in brief, with what the measure means. "The measurement of information reported is the fraction
   of correctly classified trials (chance level = 0.05 as there were 20 stimuli in each set)." Definitions
   are given in prose at the point of use, not deferred to Methods.
3. Result with the numbers, one comparison per sentence. "The mean classification performance from this
   classifier was 80%, which is higher than the performances from each of the three individual features
   (32–53%; figure 3b,c)."
4. Interpretive closer, hedged to fit. "This suggests that the rostral, higher auditory cortex does not
   represent all types of conspecific vocalizations equally." / "These results suggest that caller
   identification can be improved by combining acoustic features ..." / "Thus, the caller identity cues are
   distributed across acoustic features, and combining them could provide more information about identity."

The closer is never omitted, and it is never a restatement of the number; it says what the number means for
the question of beat 1. When a paragraph tests a prediction, beat 1 states the prediction explicitly:
"If so, then we should be able to find a relationship between an animal's weight and the similarity among
animals judged by the classifier ... Interestingly, we found that the first dimension of the MDS was highly
correlated with the animal's body weight (rho = 0.93, p = 0.0007)."

## 3. Connectives that carry the logic

Used freely and at the start of sentences: Thus, Therefore, However, In contrast, Accordingly, Indeed,
Interestingly, Note that, Henceforth, In the following, We have thus far ..., In the next analysis ...,
Conversely, Nevertheless. Each analysis is announced ("We first quantified ...", "We then used ...", "We
next examined ...", "We further found ...") so the reader always knows where the argument is. Paragraph
transitions summarise what was just shown before moving on: "Results from single acoustic features suggest
that (i) ... and (ii) .... Thus, ... and combining them could provide more information. We indeed found
that ..."

## 4. Counter-explanations are raised and dismissed in place

"This difference cannot be simply attributed to the effects of age because the ages of the birds ... overlap
with ..." / "It is important to note that there was also less power in higher frequency bands ..., and this
may account for some of our results. However, in Sector 4, ... This supports the idea that ..." / "This cannot
entirely be explained by a general reduction of evoked power ..." The alternative is named in one sentence,
the evidence against it in the next, the conclusion in a third.

## 5. Introduction shape

General fact -> the specific system -> what is established (with citations) -> "This leaves open several
questions" -> the questions, numbered and phrased as measurable ("to what extent", "what is the degree to
which") -> "Here we addressed these questions by ..." (the approach and what it enables) -> stop. No results
in the Introduction; the last paragraph names the method and its reach ("Using this approach, we could
evaluate ...").

## 6. Discussion shape

Opens with a two-to-four-sentence recap in the past tense ("In the current study, we investigated ... We
then used ... We found ..."), then relates each finding to prior work in the order of the Results, then
thematic subsections with declarative headings ("Coding of vocalization is supported by theta band
activity"), each ending in "Thus, one interpretation of our results is that ..." or a stated open question
("Whether these or still other mechanisms ... requires further investigation"; "It would be interesting to
test whether ...").

## 7. Headings

Results subsection headings are declarative findings, not topics: "Combined sites within each sector
produced better classification performance for vocalizations than individual sites"; "Systematic decrease in
classification performance from caudal to rostral sectors"; "Rapid syllable timing de-crystallization under
loud DAF"; "Enhanced discrimination for individual 'Coo' vocalizations".

## 8. Figures and numbers

Figure citations sit at the end of the clause in parentheses, often with the panel and the colour of the
trace ("Fig. 3d, cyan line"). Statistics appear in parentheses after the claim they support. Chance level and
the meaning of a metric are stated once, where the metric is introduced.

## 9. Hedging vocabulary (in order of strength used)

"demonstrates" (reserved for direct measurements), "shows", "suggests", "is consistent with", "supports
the idea that", "might reflect", "one interpretation of our results is". Mechanistic claims are always in
the last three forms.

## 10. Rules to apply when revising the manuscript

- Rebuild each Results paragraph on the four beats; where the draft packs two results into one sentence with
  a colon or semicolon, split them and give each its own closer if it needs one.
- Target a median sentence length near 24 words and no more than 10% of sentences over 40; keep number-heavy
  sentences long and interpretive sentences short.
- Open analyses with the purpose ("To test whether ..., we ..."; "We next asked whether ...") and name the
  actor.
- End every paragraph with "This shows/suggests/is consistent with ..." or "Thus, ...", stating the meaning
  for the question, not the number again.
- Announce transitions ("We have thus far ...; in the next analysis ...").
- Raise and dismiss the main alternative in place, in three sentences.
- Make Results subsection headings declarative findings.
- Keep the Introduction result-free and end it with the approach; open the Discussion with the recap.
